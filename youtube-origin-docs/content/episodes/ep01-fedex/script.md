## 1. Out for delivery [0:00-1:00]

[METER: safe]
[VIS: close-up of a hand holding a phone, the screen shows a simple map with a dotted line ending at a small house] [LABEL: "Out For Delivery"]
At some point this week, you probably refreshed a tracking page. "Out for delivery." It might be the most boring sentence on the internet.

[VIS: night sky over a huge airport, dozens of tiny cargo jets lined up like toys, runway lights glowing]
But behind that sentence is a machine that wakes up when you go to sleep. Every night, cargo jets from all over America fly into one city in Tennessee. In a few hours, millions of boxes are sorted, and the same planes fly them back out before sunrise.

[VIS: a tiny round-headed figure standing in front of a mountain of cardboard boxes that reaches the clouds] [LABEL: "14 million a day"]
On an average day in 2026, FedEx moved around fourteen million packages in the United States alone. [VERIFY: US domestic average daily package volume ~14.2M — FedEx FY2026 Q4 earnings release / 10-K; confirm whether it is a full-year or Q4 figure] That's more packages, every single day, than there are people in Belgium. [VERIFY: Belgium population ~11.9M]

[VIS: a young man at a desk in a college dorm room, writing a paper by lamplight, a small sketch gauge on the wall]
And it almost never happened. It started as a college paper that, legend says, got a C. It survived because one night, with five thousand dollars left in the bank, its founder flew to Las Vegas. Keep an eye on the meter in the corner. It shows how close this company came to dying. This is the entire history of FedEx.

## 2. The Marine with a C-grade idea [1:00-4:00]

[VIS: a small Mississippi town in the 1940s, a bus parked outside a little diner]
Frederick Wallace Smith was born in 1944, in Marks, Mississippi. [VERIFY: born 11 Aug 1944, Marks, Mississippi] His father had built a business out of buses and diners: a regional bus line that became part of Greyhound, and a chain of small restaurants called Toddle House. [VERIFY: father James Frederick Smith — Dixie Greyhound Lines and Toddle House]

[VIS: a small boy holding his mother's hand beside a tree on a grey day]
But his father died when Fred was four years old. [VERIFY: father died 1948] The boy grew up with his mother and his uncles, and with a share of the family fortune held in trust. Remember that money. One day he would bet it.

[VIS: a boy on crutches at the edge of a field, watching other kids play ball]
As a child, Fred had a bone disease in his hip, and spent years on crutches and in leg braces. [VERIFY: Calvé-Perthes disease, recovered around age 10] He recovered. And as a teenager he found the thing he loved most: flying. He was piloting small planes while most kids his age were learning to drive. [VERIFY: learned to fly at ~15]

[VIS: an ivy-covered university building, a young man carrying a tall stack of books]
In 1962, he went to Yale. And this is where the most famous part of the legend begins. [VERIFY: Yale 1962–1966]

[VIS: a young man typing at a desk, a sketch map of America pinned above him with lines pointing to one dot in the middle]
In an economics class, Smith wrote a paper about a problem he had noticed. America was filling up with computers and electronics. When a part broke, a business needed the replacement tomorrow, not next week. But packages travelled as cargo on passenger airlines. And passenger airlines flew where people wanted to go, at times that suited people, not boxes.

[VIS: a map drawn like a bicycle wheel, spokes running from many small towns into one hub in the middle] [LABEL: "Hub and spoke"]
His idea sounded backwards. Fly every package in the country to one city in the middle, in the middle of the night. Sort everything there. Then fly it all back out before morning. A box going from Boston to New York would first fly halfway across America. It sounds crazy. But it means every city connects to every other city with just two flights.

[VIS: a professor's hand writing a big letter on top of a typed paper, a coffee cup beside it] [LABEL: "A 'C'?"]
The story goes that his professor gave the paper a C. Smith himself later said he didn't remember the grade, and that it was probably his usual C. [VERIFY: Smith's own words on the grade — find an interview source] So treat the C as a legend. But the idea in that paper was the whole company, before the company existed.

[VIS: a jungle landscape, helicopters in the distance, a young officer kneeling with a map]
Then, instead of starting a business, he joined the Marines. Smith served two tours in Vietnam, first as a platoon leader, then as a forward air controller, flying with pilots on combat missions. [VERIFY: Marines 1966–1970, two tours, platoon leader, forward air controller, ~200 missions] He came home with a Silver Star, a Bronze Star and two Purple Hearts. [VERIFY: decorations]

[VIS: soldiers passing crates hand to hand from a military cargo plane]
He later said that everything he knew about running FedEx came from the Marines, not from Yale. [VERIFY: quote source, e.g. obituary coverage] He had seen up close how a huge organisation moves people and supplies under pressure, and how much depends on things arriving on time.

[VIS: a small aircraft hangar, a mechanic sitting by a phone next to a plane with an open engine]
Back home, he bought control of an aircraft maintenance company in Little Rock, Arkansas. [VERIFY: Arkansas Aviation Sales, ~1969–70] And every day he saw the problem from his term paper. A plane was stuck on the ground, waiting for a part. The part was somewhere in America. And there was no reliable way to get it there by tomorrow.

[VIS: a young man at a table, a small bag of money on one side, a sketch of a jet on the other] [METER: shaky]
So in 1971, at twenty-seven years old, Fred Smith decided to build the system himself. There was just one problem. A hub-and-spoke network only works if it is huge from day one. You can't start in one city. And that meant he needed more money than almost any startup in America had ever raised.

## 3. Fourteen jets, 186 packages [4:00-8:00]

[VIS: a young man shaking hands across a long table with bankers in dark suits]
Federal Express was founded in 1971. [VERIFY: incorporated June 1971] Even the name was a bet. Smith hoped to win a contract to fly cheques overnight between the banks of the Federal Reserve. The contract never came. The name stayed. [VERIFY: Federal Reserve contract story]

[VIS: two serious men in suits handing over thick bound reports to a young man at a desk]
Before he spent the money, Smith wanted proof. He paid two consulting firms to study one question: would American businesses pay a lot more to get a package there by tomorrow morning? Both studies came back with the same answer. Yes. [VERIFY: two demand studies — A.T. Kearney and Aviation Consultants Inc.?] One of the consultants who worked on that research, Roger Frock, liked the idea so much that he joined the company. Remember his name. He's about to tell us the strangest story in this video. [VERIFY: Roger Frock's role in the study and joining as an early executive]

[VIS: an old weighing scale, a small bag of family money on one side, a tall mountain of banknotes on the other] [LABEL: "$4 million in"]
Smith put in about four million dollars of his own family money. Then he went looking for the rest, and raised around ninety million dollars in loans and investment. [VERIFY: ~$4M own money, ~$90M raised] At the time, it was one of the largest venture-backed startups in American history. [VERIFY: "largest venture capital start-up" claim — find source]

[VIS: a giant rulebook wrapped in chains, a tiny jet trying to take off from its cover] [LABEL: "The rules"]
But there was another wall. In the early 1970s, American aviation was tightly regulated. To fly big cargo planes between cities, you needed government permission that was almost impossible to get. [VERIFY: Civil Aeronautics Board route certificates]

[VIS: a small sleek business jet with its seats being carried out, cardboard boxes stacked inside the cabin]
Smith found the loophole. Small aircraft counted as air taxis, and air taxis didn't need that permission. So Federal Express bought small French business jets, Dassault Falcon 20s, tore out the seats, cut in a cargo door, and turned them into tiny freighters. [VERIFY: air-taxi exemption + Falcon 20 cargo conversion]

[VIS: a map of the United States covered in storm clouds, except for one clear sunny dot in Tennessee]
For the hub, he chose Memphis, Tennessee. It sat close to the middle of the country. Its weather was mild, so the airport rarely shut down. And it had room to grow. [VERIFY: reasons Memphis was chosen; Little Rock airport turned the company down?]

[VIS: a night scene from above: small jets landing one after another at one airport, workers carrying boxes between them like ants]
Here is how the machine worked. In the evening, couriers picked up packages in each city. The jets flew them to Memphis, all landing around midnight. Workers unloaded every plane, sorted every box by destination, and loaded them back onto the right plane. Before dawn, the jets took off again, and by morning the packages were on their way to their doors. Every city, connected to every other city, in one night.

[VIS: a lonely airport ramp at night, a handful of boxes on a single cart] [LABEL: "6 packages"]
In March 1973, they ran a test night across a small set of cities. The planes came back with six packages. [VERIFY: trial night, 6 packages — EBSCO "Federal Express Begins Operations"] Smith pushed the official launch back.

[VIS: fourteen small jets lined up on a runway at night, workers holding flashlights] [LABEL: "April 17, 1973"]
On April the seventeenth, 1973, Federal Express officially launched. Fourteen Falcon jets. Three hundred and eighty-nine employees. Twenty-five cities. [VERIFY: 14 jets, 389 team members, 25 cities — FedEx history page; Aviation Week says 24 cities]

[VIS: a small pile of boxes on a huge empty sorting table, a few workers staring at it] [LABEL: "186 packages"]
That first night, they delivered one hundred and eighty-six packages. [VERIFY: 186 packages] Spread across fourteen jets, that's about thirteen packages per plane.

[VIS: an empty bridge over a river, a single tiny car driving across it]
That sounds like a disaster. But think of it like a bridge. You have to build the whole bridge before the first car can cross. Smith had built a national network before he had national customers. Now he had to find them, fast.

[VIS: sunrise over a small office building, a courier handing a box to a smiling receptionist]
But here's the thing. It worked. Packages that went to sleep in one city woke up in another. Customers who tried it came back. Night after night, the numbers grew. A few hundred packages. Then a few thousand.

[VIS: a young man pointing at a chalkboard with a rising line drawn on it, colleagues watching]
Smith was right about the idea. America did want overnight delivery. Businesses would pay extra for "tomorrow." The hub did work.

[VIS: an accountant's ledger with red ink running down the page and dripping off the desk] [METER: near-death] [LABEL: "Losing money"]
There was just one problem. Every night, those jets were flying across the country half empty. And every night, the company lost money. The idea worked. The math didn't.

## 4. $5,000 and a night in Las Vegas [8:00-12:00]

[VIS: a long line of cars at a gas station, a hand-drawn fuel pump with a big X on it]
Then, in October 1973, the world changed under their feet. Arab oil producers cut supplies to the United States, and the price of oil shot up. [VERIFY: 1973 oil embargo, price roughly quadrupled] For an airline that burned jet fuel every single night, it was a disaster.

[VIS: a stack of dollar bills shaped like a jet engine, burning at the back] [LABEL: "~$1 million a month"]
Federal Express was now losing around a million dollars a month. [VERIFY: monthly losses in 1973–74] The ninety million was disappearing. Investors were nervous. Banks didn't want to lend more. And suppliers wanted to be paid.

[VIS: a desk at night under a single lamp, a bank statement showing a very small number] [LABEL: "$5,000 left"]
This is where the most famous story in FedEx history happens. As Roger Frock, one of Smith's first executives, later told it, the company had about five thousand dollars in the bank. [VERIFY: Roger Frock, "Changing How the World Does Business" (2006)] And a fuel bill of around twenty-four thousand dollars was due on Monday. [VERIFY: $24,000 fuel bill] No fuel meant no flights. No flights meant no company.

[VIS: a tall corporate tower, a small figure walking out of the front door with his head down]
Smith had flown to Chicago to ask General Dynamics, the aircraft and defence company, for money. They said no. [VERIFY: General Dynamics meeting, Chicago]

[VIS: an airport gate, a small plane taking off toward glowing desert lights on the horizon]
So instead of flying home to Memphis, he got on a plane to Las Vegas.

[VIS: a round-headed man alone at a blackjack table, a dealer turning cards, a very small stack of chips] [LABEL: "Blackjack"]
He sat down at a blackjack table with the company's last few thousand dollars. And he won.

[VIS: the same man on a Monday morning placing a cheque on a desk in front of two stunned colleagues] [LABEL: "$27,000"]
On Monday morning, the company had about twenty-seven thousand dollars. Enough to pay the fuel bill. [VERIFY: $27,000] When Frock asked where the money came from, Smith reportedly told him the meeting had been a bust, so he went to Vegas. [VERIFY: Frock's account of the conversation]

[VIS: a calendar with pages being torn off fast, one by one]
In later years, Smith played the story down. The winnings didn't save the company on their own. They bought a few days. [VERIFY: Smith's later comments that it was "not decisive"] But a few days was exactly what Federal Express needed. Smith kept raising money, round after round, often on worse and worse terms. Each round bought a few more months.

[VIS: a courtroom sketch, a small figure standing before a high judge's bench] [LABEL: "Charged"]
And the pressure wasn't only about money. Smith was charged with forging documents to get a bank loan guaranteed by his family's company. He argued he had the authority to sign them, and a jury acquitted him. [VERIFY: indictment and acquittal, 1975 — details and dates] His own sisters took him to court over the family money he had put into the business. [VERIFY: sisters' lawsuit and how it ended]

[VIS: a pilot at night handing a credit card to a fuel truck driver beside a small jet]
Inside the company, the stories became legend. Pilots, it's said, sometimes paid for fuel with their own credit cards. Employees held on to their paychecks so they wouldn't bounce. [VERIFY: find a primary source (Frock's book / interviews) — cut if not found] Whether every detail is true or not, everyone who was there tells the same story. For about two years, Federal Express lived week to week.

[VIS: a young man sitting alone at the end of a long boardroom table, older investors in suits staring at him]
Every new round of money came with strings attached. The investors now owned most of the company, and they were losing patience with the man running it. Smith was not yet thirty. He had put his family's money in, his name on the loans, and his reputation on the line. If the company died, all of it died with it. [VERIFY: investors' control and pressure on Smith's role, 1974–75 — find a source or soften]

[VIS: a hand-drawn gauge whose needle is swinging back from red toward yellow] [METER: shaky] [LABEL: "First profit"]
Then, slowly, the line on the chart turned. Here is the strange math of a hub. The planes fly every night whether they carry thirteen packages or thirteen hundred. So once the network exists, every extra package costs almost nothing to carry. As more businesses discovered overnight delivery, the same machine that had been burning money started to make it. By 1975, Federal Express was finally profitable. [VERIFY: first profit — month/year and figure] The company that should have died more than once was still flying.

[VIS: a tiny jet inside a birdcage made of rulebook pages]
But it was still flying small jets. The rules that had forced Smith to buy tiny Falcons were still in place. And as long as they stayed, Federal Express could never grow big enough to fill its own idea.

## 5. "FedEx it" [12:00-17:00]

[VIS: a grand government building with long steps, a small figure with a briefcase climbing them] [METER: safe]
So Smith went to Washington. He spent years arguing that cargo airlines should be free to fly bigger planes. In November 1977, the law changed, and air cargo was deregulated. [VERIFY: air cargo deregulation, signed Nov 1977]

[VIS: a huge cargo jet towering over a tiny business jet, like a whale next to a small fish]
Almost overnight, Federal Express could buy real cargo jets. Boeing 727s first, then giant wide-body DC-10s. [VERIFY: 727s from 1977–78, DC-10s from ~1980] The half-empty planes of 1973 became full planes, flying routes the old airlines had never thought of.

[VIS: a busy stock exchange floor, tiny traders throwing paper in the air] [LABEL: "1978"]
In 1978, Federal Express went public on the stock market. [VERIFY: IPO April 1978; NYSE listing date] The man who had been days from collapse was suddenly very rich.

[VIS: a 1980s television in a living room, on screen a man in a suit talking so fast that speech lines fly everywhere] [LABEL: "Absolutely, positively"]
Then came the advertising. A slogan America still remembers: "When it absolutely, positively has to be there overnight." [VERIFY: slogan year and agency] And a TV ad with a man who talked so fast you could barely follow him, playing an executive in a hurry. [VERIFY: "Fast Paced World" ad, John Moschitta Jr., 1981] The message was simple. Business moves fast. Federal Express moves faster.

[VIS: a staircase of stacked boxes rising into the clouds, a tiny man at the top waving a flag] [LABEL: "$1 billion"]
By 1983, Federal Express was making a billion dollars a year in revenue. It's often described as the first American company to hit that mark within ten years of starting, without buying other companies. [VERIFY: $1B revenue in 1983 and the "first US company" claim]

[VIS: an early 1980s office machine glowing like treasure on a pedestal, then the same machine covered in cobwebs] [METER: shaky]
But success made Smith bold, and boldness led to the company's second big mistake. In 1984, Federal Express launched ZapMail. Documents were sent electronically between its offices, then printed and delivered by courier within two hours. [VERIFY: ZapMail launch 1984, two-hour promise] It was clever. It was also beaten by something smaller: the cheap office fax machine. Why pay a courier when you could fax from your own desk?

[VIS: a pile of money being swept into a dustbin by a giant broom] [LABEL: "ZapMail"]
ZapMail was shut down in 1986. The losses ran into the hundreds of millions of dollars. [VERIFY: closed Oct 1986; write-off figures differ — ~$320M (Wikipedia) vs ~$340M pretax (UPI) vs ~$190M after tax — pick one, say "pre-tax", cite it] For most young companies, that would have been the end. For Federal Express, the core business was now strong enough to take the hit.

[VIS: a courier scanning a box with a chunky handheld scanner, dotted lines connecting the scanner to a distant computer] [METER: safe]
And while ZapMail failed, another idea quietly changed everything. Federal Express had been building computer systems to follow every package through the network. In the 1980s, couriers started carrying handheld scanners, and every box was scanned at every step. [VERIFY: COSMOS tracking system; handheld "SuperTracker" scanners, mid-1980s] This is the real origin of the tracking page you refreshed this week.

[VIS: a world map with cargo jets fanning out from America across the oceans]
In 1989, Federal Express bought Flying Tiger Line, an international cargo airline, and with it the right to land in airports around the world. [VERIFY: Flying Tigers acquisition 1989, price ~$880M] The overnight network now crossed oceans.

[VIS: one person handing a box to another, a speech bubble shaped like an arrow between them] [LABEL: "FedEx it"]
By the early 1990s, customers had already renamed the company. Nobody said "send it by Federal Express." They said "FedEx it." So in 1994, the company made it official. Federal Express became FedEx. [VERIFY: 1994 rebrand]

[VIS: two big blocky shapes side by side with a hidden white arrow in the gap between them, a tiny figure pointing at it in surprise] [LABEL: "The hidden arrow"]
The new logo came with a secret. Look at the white space between the "E" and the "x." There's an arrow, pointing forward. [VERIFY: logo by Lindon Leader at Landor, 1994] Once you see it, you can never unsee it. That same year, FedEx let customers track their own packages on its website, one of the first big companies to do it. [VERIFY: fedex.com online tracking launched 1994]

[VIS: a man on a tiny desert island holding an unopened package, a ball with a painted face sitting beside him]
In 2000, FedEx got the kind of publicity money can't buy. In the film Cast Away, Tom Hanks plays a FedEx systems engineer stranded on an island, who never opens the last package. [VERIFY: Cast Away (2000); FedEx said it did not pay for placement]

[VIS: a long white delivery truck pulling up to a small suburban house, a family waiting at the door]
But overnight jets were expensive, and not everything needed to arrive by tomorrow. In 1998, the company bought Caliber System, whose RPS ground network became FedEx Ground. [VERIFY: Caliber System acquisition 1998; RPS renamed FedEx Ground in 2000] Now FedEx had trucks as well as planes. And just in time. A new kind of customer was about to order everything to their front door.

[VIS: a blue street mailbox with a cargo jet flying low over it]
A year later, FedEx signed a deal to fly mail for the US Postal Service. [VERIFY: 2001 USPS air transportation agreement] The company that once wasn't allowed to fly big planes was now carrying the government's own mail.

[VIS: a gigantic sorting building seen from above at night, conveyor belts like rivers of boxes, tiny workers] [LABEL: "Memphis, every night"]
And every night, the heart of it all was still Memphis. The FedEx hub became one of the largest sorting operations on Earth, and helped make Memphis one of the busiest cargo airports in the world. [VERIFY: Memphis cargo airport ranking (No. 1 for years; current rank)] The idea from a term paper had become a city's economy.

[VIS: an enormous warehouse casting a long shadow over a small delivery truck] [METER: shaky]
By the late 2010s, FedEx's biggest threat wasn't its old rival UPS. It was one of its own biggest customers.

## 6. The customer who became a rival [17:00-20:00]

[VIS: a quiet neighbourhood street where every single doorstep has a cardboard box on it]
Online shopping should have been the best thing that ever happened to FedEx. And in many ways it was. Instead of a few big business customers, there were now millions of homes, each waiting for a box. But a box to a house is harder to deliver than a box to an office. Houses are spread out. Nobody is home to sign. And online shops push hard for the lowest price.

[VIS: a cargo jet at dawn being loaded with small cold boxes marked with a snowflake]
Then, in 2020, the pandemic turned home delivery into a lifeline. Package volumes jumped. And when the first COVID-19 vaccines were shipped across the United States that December, FedEx planes carried them. [VERIFY: Dec 2020 — FedEx and UPS shipped the first vaccine doses] The company built for "tomorrow morning" was now moving medicine a country was waiting for.

[VIS: a fleet of plain vans with no markings pouring out of a giant warehouse]
But one customer was quietly building something else. Amazon had spent years building its own delivery network. Its own planes. Its own vans. Its own drivers. In 2019, FedEx walked away from its Amazon delivery contracts. [VERIFY: FedEx ended Express contract (June 2019) and Ground contract (Aug 2019)] Today, Amazon delivers more parcels in the US than FedEx does. [VERIFY: Pitney Bowes Parcel Shipping Index — latest year] The customer had become the competitor.

[VIS: an older round-headed man handing a small paper airplane to a younger man]
In 2022, Fred Smith stepped down as CEO and handed the company to Raj Subramaniam, staying on as executive chairman. [VERIFY: CEO change effective June 1, 2022] In June 2025, Fred Smith died, at the age of eighty. [VERIFY: died 21 June 2025] He had spent more than fifty years running the machine that started as a college paper.

[VIS: a large company building being carefully split in two by a giant saw, both halves still standing]
FedEx is changing shape again. It has been merging its air and ground networks to cut costs. [VERIFY: Express and Ground combined into one company in 2024; "Network 2.0"] And on June the first, 2026, it spun off its trucking business, FedEx Freight, as a separate company on the stock market. [VERIFY: spin-off completed 1 June 2026, NYSE: FDXF]

[VIS: a tiny figure standing in front of an enormous stack of coins] [LABEL: "$94.7 billion"]
So how big is the machine today? In its 2026 financial year, FedEx reported about ninety-four point seven billion dollars in revenue. [VERIFY: FY ending 31 May 2026 revenue $94.7B — check whether recast figures excluding Freight differ] It flies the world's largest all-cargo air fleet. [VERIFY: "world's largest all-cargo air fleet" — FedEx 10-K] And every working day, it still moves around fourteen million packages across America.

[VIS: the phone from the opening, the tracking map now shows a tick next to the small house] [LABEL: "Delivered"] [METER: off]
So the next time you see "out for delivery," think about what's behind it. A paper that might have got a C. A Marine who came home from war and saw a problem nobody else wanted to solve. Fourteen tiny jets carrying one hundred and eighty-six packages. And a weekend in Las Vegas with five thousand dollars on the table.

Here's my question for you. Fred Smith's gamble worked. If it hadn't, we would call it the worst decision in business history. So was it genius, or just luck? Tell me in the comments.
